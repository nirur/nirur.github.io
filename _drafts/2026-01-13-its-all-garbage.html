What ensues is bound to be highly opinionated. Take everything with a grain of salt.

***

The things I aim to complain about, in no particular order, are:

  • The mediocrity of ideas on blogs and, more generally, shallowness of opinionated stances. People rush to have an opinion about something and often make their decisions on superficial grounds, rather than diving deep. [Linux has forced me away from this habit in myself.]
  • Fluff text, and the fact that people get away writing it. In order to give life to mediocre ideas, people tend to over-explain them to the point that their essence is either diluted or hidden behind a wall of text. For one, rebuttals of these ideas get lost somewhere along the way: the few people capable of forming a rebuttal get tired reading, and the rest of the world is left in the dark.
  • Most people aren't thinking critically about what they read. In other words, writing style matters more than writing content. I agree, writing style is reasonably important from the standpoint of a writer looking to be heard, but the reader shouldn't be overly wooed by writing style alone. (Some famous poets, in my opinion, do way too much of this. I'll elaborate later on, when I get to the circle-jerking of writers.)
  • An example of the previous is over-glorification. Some people say good things for the sake of speaking well about someone, someplace, or some event. Once you see a few of their posts, all written in the same tone, in the same format (some version of "I used to think ___" followed by "I was completely wrong, and I realized this when ___", all tied together by "[that event] was the greatest thing that happened to me and will happen to me in all of eternity") you begin to realize they're writing things that make the reader feel good. The overused catch-phrases for literary effect start to feel monotonous and the message seems shallow. What do you really think? What would you say if your friends at the event weren't ever to read this blog post? I've certainly had my fair share of events that, while glorified by everyone who attended, felt only a twinge above average.
  • This leads to the generation of a large mass of text with low-quality ideas that people hail as godly. Once a few people like the post (having been wooed by the writing), a flywheel forms, and the post is upvoted to oblivion. As a result, the good ideas get lost in the muck.
  • Many writing critiques have a habit of circle-jerking. The value of a literary work is in the eyes of the reader: an essay that presents a well-thought, radical idea but is poorly formatted ("your thesis statement isn't specific enough!") will earn a worse grade than a bland, rule-conforming essay. (As for the comment about poets from earlier: people who've studied literature tend to hyper-analyze works. When the author has been highly intentional, this may end well: the analyses of subtle details match the takeaways of the readers. However, with some poets [I won't name names on the internet, but feel free to ask me about this in person] their poems come off to me as vague. This entails that the poet, unbeknownst to the analysts, can write shallow things, like handing the analysts a random patch of land in the midwest, and the analysts will dig as deep as they need to in order to find some value, even if it's by reaching the mantle below and cooling the magma into obsidian. Many are wooed by the writing of these poets but, to me, it reads as a lot of sentence fragments the writer didn't take the time to expand on. I get a mouthful of literary effect, a sense of there being not enough intentionality, and an unclear picture of the quality of the idea in the author's mind.)
  • This results in school systems pumping out a uniform mass of well-structured essays. Some students are good at school, and others can find novel ideas. The only novel ideas that have a shot at reaching a large audience come from students who are both, those that realize school is a hindrance, a petty obstacle in the way of recognition of their true merit. They're good at school not because they see it as useful, but because their worth is only recognized if they are.
  • Indeed, literature circles are fascinated with people who have the skill to conform to the norm, but choose not to. Walt Whitman and Emily Dickinson are two examples. But, what about all the other radical thinkers who couldn't mold their ideas to fit the canned 5 Paragraph Essay™ format?
  • Among these rare thinkers, some have given up trying to explain their routine ideas at all. They've given up trying to compile their thoughts into human-readable format. An example of this? Some cracked MIT student. [Again, won't name names on the internet.] We see the consequences of over-pedanticity: we're at the whims of the effort the thinker wants to put into conveying their ideas.
  • There's also the hindrance of explanation. Let me explain. If I simply state an idea, as I have been doing all post long, you the reader are probably not going to side with me immediately. To generate some interest, I need to take a long-winded path of giving examples, motivating each sub-result, and putting the idea on a firm basis. I have no problem doing this, if only I were ensured that the listener would actually be listening to me raving until the very end. I wouldn't have any problem doing this if the listener would wait for me to formulate my claim, collect evidence, ramble through the explanation, and finally provide a well-thought critique. In practice, the acquaintance will drop the conversation and talk to someone else before I'm done planning my response. The friend may wait, but lose interest soon after I start talking. This blog is the only one that'll bear with me until I can get the words out.
  • Last, there's the popular belief that writing helps you think in some way. I contest that it usually doesn't generate as high-quality of a raw insight; it does, though, help bring an already thought-out, self-consistent principle into full focus. Let's take the example of math for a moment.

Bloggers are rewarded by their readers for pushing content regularly, for publishing things punctually and for making it sound interesting. This leads to an obscene amount of ``fluff text,'' wherein mediocre, half-formed stubs of ideas are fleshed out into paragraphs' worth of text.
